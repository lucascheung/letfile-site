Section 24 for landlords, explained simply

The rule that stopped landlords deducting their mortgage interest. Here's what Section 24 actually does to your tax bill, who it hits hardest, and how it shows up under Making Tax Digital.

UK residential landlords · income tax · last reviewed November 2026

If you're a landlord with a mortgage, Section 24 is probably the single biggest change to your tax in the last decade, and one of the most misunderstood. In short: you can no longer treat your mortgage interest as a normal expense.

What Section 24 actually is

Section 24 (sometimes called the "tenant tax" or the finance cost restriction) stops individual residential landlords deducting finance costs from their rental profit. Finance costs mainly means mortgage interest, but also interest on loans for furnishings and fees for arranging a loan.

Instead of deducting them, you get a basic-rate tax credit worth 20% of your finance costs. It was phased in from 2017 and has been fully in force since 6 April 2020.

The one-sentence version

You're now taxed on your rental profit before deducting mortgage interest, then your tax bill is cut by 20% of that interest. For a basic-rate taxpayer that's roughly neutral; for a higher-rate taxpayer it's a real increase.

Who it hits, and who it doesn't

  • Basic-rate (20%) taxpayers: broadly unaffected, you'd have got relief at 20% anyway.
  • Higher-rate (40%) and additional-rate (45%) taxpayers: worse off. You used to deduct interest at your marginal rate; now you only get 20% back.
  • The hidden sting: because your taxable profit is now calculated without deducting interest, your income looks higher. That can tip you into the higher-rate band, start tapering your personal allowance above £100,000, or trigger the High Income Child Benefit Charge, even if your real cash profit hasn't changed.
  • Not affected: limited companies (they still deduct interest normally), and commercial property letting.

A worked example

A higher-rate landlord receives £20,000 rent, pays £9,000 mortgage interest and £3,000 other costs.

 The old way (pre-2020)With Section 24
Taxable rental profit£8,000£17,000
Tax at 40%£3,200£6,800
Less 20% credit on £9,000 interest–−£1,800
Tax due£3,200£5,000

Same rent, same mortgage, same costs, but £1,800 more tax under Section 24. That gap is why the rule matters so much to leveraged landlords.

What it means under Making Tax Digital

Under MTD for Income Tax you report your figures to HMRC every quarter. Your residential finance costs are captured as their own line, and HMRC applies the 20% credit in the tax calculation. You don't do the maths by hand, but you do need to record the interest correctly each period so the credit is right.

Section 24 and jointly owned property

If you own the property with someone else, each owner reports their own share of the finance costs and gets the 20% credit on that share, because joint owners file separately. Splitting the interest correctly between you matters for both of your tax bills.

Frequently asked questions

What counts as a finance cost?
Mortgage interest is the big one, plus interest on loans to buy furnishings for the property and fees incurred in getting a loan. The restriction is on the interest, not the capital repayment.
Does Section 24 apply to limited companies?
No. Companies that hold property still deduct mortgage interest as a normal business expense. That's one reason some landlords incorporate, though that has its own costs and tax consequences, so take advice first.
Is the credit always 20% of my interest?
Usually, but strictly it's the lowest of three figures: 20% of your finance costs, 20% of your property profits, or 20% of your income above your personal allowance. For most landlords it works out as 20% of the interest, with any unused amount carried forward.
Do furnished holiday lets still get full relief?
No. The separate furnished holiday lettings tax regime was abolished from April 2025, so these properties are now treated like other residential lets for finance-cost relief. Check your own position on GOV.UK.
