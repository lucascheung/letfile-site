Making Tax Digital for jointly owned property

If you own a rental property with someone else, Making Tax Digital doesn't treat you as one landlord. It treats you as two. Here's exactly what that means, and how to file it without doing everything twice by hand.

UK Making Tax Digital for Income Tax · written for jointly-owned (couple and co-owned) property · last reviewed November 2026

Making Tax Digital for Income Tax (MTD ITSA) is now live. From 6 April 2026 it applies to landlords whose combined gross property and self-employment income is over £50,000; the threshold falls to £30,000 from April 2027 and £20,000 from April 2028. If that's you, you no longer file one Self Assessment a year. You keep digital records and send HMRC four quarterly updates plus a final declaration every year.

That's a big enough change on its own. But if the property is jointly owned, there's a catch almost nobody spells out clearly.

The core rule: each owner files their own share

Under MTD, a jointly-owned property is not filed once by "the household". Each co-owner is a separate taxpayer who reports their own share of the income and expenses, under their own National Insurance number, through their own HMRC authorisation.

So a couple who own one flat 50/50 don't have five filings between them. They have ten: four quarterly updates and a final declaration each. Every rent receipt and every expense has to be split down the middle and reported twice.

In plain numbers

One jointly-owned property, two owners, equals 2 sets of digital records, 8 quarterly updates and 2 final declarations a year. Miss any one of them and HMRC's points-based penalty system starts ticking.

How do you split the income between joint owners?

You split by beneficial ownership share, the share of the property each person actually owns. There are two common situations:

Your situationDefault split for tax
Married couple or civil partners, jointly owned50/50 automatically, whatever the deeds say, unless you file a Form 17 election
Unmarried co-owners (partners, friends, family, business partners)Your actual beneficial ownership shares (for example 60/40 if that's how you own it)

Once you know the split, you apply it to every figure: rent, letting agent fees, repairs, insurance, mortgage interest, the lot. Each owner reports only their slice.

Form 17: when married couples don't want 50/50

If you're married or in a civil partnership and you genuinely own the property in unequal shares (say 75/25), you can make a Form 17 election to be taxed on those actual shares instead of the default 50/50. It only works where the unequal beneficial ownership is real and evidenced, and both of you must sign it. Many couples never need it; but if one partner is a higher-rate taxpayer, getting the split right matters.

Section 24 applies to each owner separately

For residential property you can't simply deduct mortgage interest from your rental profit. Under Section 24, finance costs instead give you a basic-rate (20%) tax credit. Because joint owners file separately, that credit is worked out on each owner's own share of the interest. Get the apportionment wrong and you can misstate both owners' tax.

Worked example

A couple own a flat 50/50. In a quarter they receive £7,200 rent, pay £1,600 running costs and £2,400 mortgage interest. Each owner reports £3,600 rent, £800 costs and £1,200 interest, and each gets the Section 24 credit on their own £1,200. Two filings, every number halved, every quarter.

Do we both have to authorise HMRC?

Yes. HMRC requires each taxpayer to grant their own authorisation once (you sign in with your own Government Gateway and consent). After that, the actual quarterly filing can be done by one person for both owners, you don't both have to log in every quarter. But the consent itself is personal and can't be delegated away.

The honest problem for couples

Most MTD software is built for a single taxpayer with a single set of books. It has no concept of "this property is owned by two people who each need their own return". So couples are left to either:

  • run two separate software subscriptions and hand-split every figure between them, or
  • pay an accountant to do the apportionment (typically £150 to £300+ a year, each), or
  • keep a spreadsheet and hope the halves reconcile.

All three are more work, more cost and more chance of a slip, four times a year, forever.

Frequently asked questions

If we own a property jointly, do we both have to do MTD?
Yes. Each co-owner is a separate taxpayer and reports their own share of the rental income and expenses. If you own 50/50, you each file your own quarterly updates and your own final declaration, so one jointly-owned property means two complete sets of MTD filings.
How is the rental income split between joint owners?
By beneficial ownership share. Married couples and civil partners are taxed 50/50 by default unless they file a Form 17 election for their actual unequal shares. Unmarried co-owners are taxed on their actual ownership shares.
What is a Form 17 election?
It lets married couples and civil partners who own a property in unequal shares elect to be taxed on those actual shares instead of the default 50/50. It only applies where the unequal beneficial ownership is genuine and evidenced, and both partners must sign it.
Does Section 24 apply to each owner?
Yes. Residential mortgage interest isn't deducted from profit; each owner instead gets a basic-rate (20%) tax credit on their own share of the finance costs, because each co-owner files separately.
Can one of us file for both?
The filing can be done by one person, but each owner must authorise HMRC personally once (HMRC requires each taxpayer's own consent). After that, one account files the quarterly updates for both. LetFile is built to do exactly this.
Am I even affected by MTD yet?
MTD ITSA applies from April 2026 at £50,000 of combined gross property and self-employment income, April 2027 at £30,000, and April 2028 at £20,000. Use the checker on our homepage to see your own date.
